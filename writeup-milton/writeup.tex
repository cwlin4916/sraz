\section{A controlled study of pure Monte Carlo tree search for symbolic regression}
\label{sec:pure-mcts-study}

\subsection{Motivation and scope}

The purpose of this study is to characterize the finite-budget limitations of
pure Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) in grammar-based symbolic regression.
We deliberately exclude neural policy and value networks.  The neural-guidance
question is scientifically separate: before asking whether a network improves
search, one must determine what information the underlying pure-search
procedure estimates and when that information is aligned with symbolic
optimization.

The central distinction is between the quality of a typical random completion
and the quality of the best reachable completion.  Let \(s\) be a partial
derivation and let \(\mathcal E(s)\) denote the set of terminal expressions
reachable from \(s\).  The best-achievable value is
\begin{equation}
    V^*(s)
    =
    \max_{\pi\in\mathcal E(s)} R(\pi),
    \label{eq:vstar}
\end{equation}
where \(R(\pi)\) is the terminal symbolic-regression reward.  By contrast,
consider the fixed random-completion policy that, at each nonterminal state
\(s\), selects uniformly among the legal actions \(\mathcal A(s)\),
\[
    q(a\mid s)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal A(s)|},
    \qquad a\in\mathcal A(s).
\]
Because transitions are deterministic, following this policy from \(s\) to
termination induces a distribution \(q(\pi\mid s)\) over the completions
\(\pi\in\mathcal E(s)\), and the mean-rollout value is
\begin{equation}
    V^q(s)
    =
    \mathbb E_{\pi\sim q(\cdot\mid s)}
    \bigl[R(\pi)\bigr].
    \label{eq:vroll}
\end{equation}
The optimization objective depends on \(V^*\), whereas conventional UCT uses
empirical mean returns as its exploitation statistic
\cite{kocsis2006bandit}.  Asymptotic consistency does not by itself imply
effective finite-budget search, and adverse finite-depth behavior is known for
UCT \cite{coquelin2007bandit}.  This distinction is particularly relevant to
symbolic regression, where the goal is to discover at least one exceptional
expression rather than a policy with high average return.  Best-focused Monte
Carlo methods have a history in single-player search and expression discovery
\cite{cazenave2009nested,cazenave2013montecarlo}, while risk-seeking symbolic
regression explicitly optimizes upper-tail rather than expected performance
\cite{petersen2021deep}.  Recent MCTS-based symbolic-regression systems also
emphasize learned proposal mechanisms or extreme-value allocation rather than
pure mean-based UCT \cite{kamienny2023deep,huang2025improving}.

We isolate two potential sources of difficulty:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{value-semantic failure}: \(V^q\) ranks competing branches in
    the opposite order from \(V^*\); formally, for two sibling states $s_1$ and $s_2$, a value-semantic failure exists if:$$V^q(s_1) > V^q(s_2) \quad \text{while} \quad V^*(s_1) < V^*(s_2)$$In this scenario, MCTS confidently exploits the wrong branch because a shallow, suboptimal "decoy" yields a high expected reward, while the branch containing the true global optimum is penalized by a low expected reward due to the abundance of poor completions.
    \item \emph{rare-event failure}: near-optimal completions have very small
    probability under the rollout policy. 
    For a tolerance $\varepsilon \geq 0$, this is the probability that a random rollout yields an expression within $\varepsilon$ of the true optimum:$$\rho_\varepsilon(s) = \Pr_{\pi \sim q(\cdot \mid s)} \big[ R(\pi) \geq V^*(s) - \varepsilon \big]$$ 


\end{enumerate}

\Cref{tab:failure-modes} summarizes the two mechanisms.

\subsection{Empirical function space}

Let
\[
    x_1,\ldots,x_n\in[-1,1],
    \qquad n=41,
\]
be equally spaced sample points, and define the empirical inner product
\begin{equation}
    \langle f,g\rangle_n
    =
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)g(x_i).
    \label{eq:empirical-inner-product}
\end{equation}
Starting from the monomials \(x,x^2,\ldots,x^{12}\), remove their empirical
means and apply Gram--Schmidt orthogonalization, 
\[
    \psi_1,\ldots,\psi_{12}
\]
satisfying
\begin{equation}
    \langle \psi_j,1\rangle_n=0,
    \qquad
    \langle\psi_j,\psi_\ell\rangle_n=\delta_{j\ell}.
    \label{eq:orthonormal-basis}
\end{equation}
The first two basis functions are the normalized linear and centered-quadratic
components,
\begin{equation}
    \psi_1(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{\mu_2}},
    \qquad
    \psi_2(x)=\frac{x^2-\mu_2}{\sqrt{\nu}},
    \label{eq:first-two-basis}
\end{equation}
where
\[
    \mu_2=\langle x,x\rangle_n,
    \qquad
    \nu=\langle x^2-\mu_2,x^2-\mu_2\rangle_n.
\]
Thus the construction extends the orthogonal linear--quadratic decomposition
used in the motivating quadratic example.

\subsection{Controlled target family}

For \(\lambda\in(0,1)\), define the four-component target
\begin{equation}
    y_\lambda(x)
    =
    \sqrt{\lambda}\,\psi_1(x)
    +
    \sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda}{3}}
    \left(
        \psi_2(x)+\psi_3(x)+\psi_4(x)
    \right).
    \label{eq:target-family}
\end{equation}
\begin{prop}[Unit target variance]
\label{prop:target-normalization}
For every \(\lambda\in(0,1)\),
\begin{equation}
    \overline y_\lambda=\langle y_\lambda,1\rangle_n=0,
    \qquad
    \|y_\lambda\|_n^2=\langle y_\lambda,y_\lambda\rangle_n=1.
    \label{eq:target-normalization}
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Both identities use only the three properties of the basis
\eqref{eq:orthonormal-basis}: each \(\psi_j\) is centered,
\(\langle\psi_j,1\rangle_n=0\); has unit length,
\(\langle\psi_j,\psi_j\rangle_n=1\); and is orthogonal to the others,
\(\langle\psi_j,\psi_\ell\rangle_n=0\) for \(j\neq\ell\).
For the mean, linearity gives
\[
    \langle y_\lambda,1\rangle_n
    =
    \sqrt{\lambda}\,\langle\psi_1,1\rangle_n
    +
    \sqrt{\tfrac{1-\lambda}{3}}
    \sum_{m=2}^{4}\langle\psi_m,1\rangle_n
    =0.
\]
Since the mean is zero, \(\|y_\lambda\|_n^2\) is the empirical variance of
\(y_\lambda\).  Expanding \(\langle y_\lambda,y_\lambda\rangle_n\) produces
sixteen terms; orthogonality annihilates every cross-term, and unit length
reduces each surviving square to its coefficient:
\[
    \|y_\lambda\|_n^2
    =
    \lambda
    +
    3\cdot\frac{1-\lambda}{3}
    =1.
\]
The identity is the Pythagorean theorem in the geometry
\eqref{eq:empirical-inner-product}.
\end{proof}

\noi The parameter \(\lambda\) is therefore exactly the fraction of target
variance carried by \(\psi_1\).  We use
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{\mathrm{weak}}=\frac14,
    \qquad
    \lambda_{\mathrm{strong}}=0.997.
    \label{eq:lambda-values}
\end{equation}
When \(\lambda=1/4\), all four target components have equal variance share.
When \(\lambda=0.997\), the first component explains \(99.7\%\) of the target
variance and each remaining component explains \(0.1\%\).  The latter is a
controlled analogue of a pseudo-linear target: a shallow one-term expression
is almost perfectly predictive but is not structurally exact.

\subsection{Controlled derivation grammar}

The root state \(s_0\) has two legal actions.

\paragraph{Decoy action.} The first action, denoted \(D\), terminates
immediately with the shallow model class
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal M_D
    =
    \operatorname{span}\{1,\psi_1\}.
    \label{eq:decoy-model}
\end{equation}
The branch contains a single terminal expression, fitted by least squares
and scored by the same unpenalized \(R^2\) criterion as every terminal
expression, cf.\ \eqref{eq:r2-reward} below.  Every visit therefore
returns one deterministic reward, and that reward is exactly the decoy
parameter.

\begin{prop}[Decoy reward]
\label{prop:decoy-reward}
For every \(\lambda\in(0,1)\), the least-squares fit of \(y_\lambda\) in
\(\mathcal M_D\) is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat y_D=\sqrt{\lambda}\,\psi_1,
    \label{eq:decoy-fit}
\end{equation}
and its reward is
\begin{equation}
    R(D)=\lambda.
    \label{eq:decoy-reward}
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Because the numerator and denominator of \eqref{eq:r2-reward} carry the
same factor \(n\), the ratio is unchanged if both are computed in the
geometry \eqref{eq:empirical-inner-product}; we do so throughout.  The
constant function satisfies \(\langle 1,1\rangle_n=1\), so by
\eqref{eq:orthonormal-basis} the set \(\{1,\psi_1\}\) is an orthonormal
basis of \(\mathcal M_D\), and the least-squares fit is the orthogonal
projection
\[
    \widehat y_D
    =
    \langle y_\lambda,1\rangle_n\,1
    +
    \langle y_\lambda,\psi_1\rangle_n\,\psi_1 .
\]
The intercept vanishes, \(\langle y_\lambda,1\rangle_n=0\), by
Proposition~\ref{prop:target-normalization}.  For the slope, expand
\(y_\lambda\) by \eqref{eq:target-family}; orthogonality annihilates the
three cross-terms and unit length leaves
\[
    \langle y_\lambda,\psi_1\rangle_n
    =
    \sqrt{\lambda}\,\langle\psi_1,\psi_1\rangle_n
    =
    \sqrt{\lambda},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:decoy-fit}.  The residual is then explicit,
\[
    y_\lambda-\widehat y_D
    =
    \sqrt{\tfrac{1-\lambda}{3}}
    \left(\psi_2+\psi_3+\psi_4\right),
\]
and the Pythagorean theorem in the geometry
\eqref{eq:empirical-inner-product} gives
\(\|y_\lambda-\widehat y_D\|_n^2=3\cdot\tfrac{1-\lambda}{3}=1-\lambda\).
Since \(\overline y_\lambda=0\) and \(\|y_\lambda\|_n^2=1\)
(Proposition~\ref{prop:target-normalization}), the reward
\eqref{eq:r2-reward} evaluates to
\[
    R(D)
    =
    1-\frac{\|y_\lambda-\widehat y_D\|_n^2}{\|y_\lambda\|_n^2}
    =
    1-(1-\lambda)
    =
    \lambda.
    \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{rem}[The decoy is an exact trap]
\label{rem:decoy-trap}
Proposition~\ref{prop:decoy-reward} is classical: on orthonormal
regressors, least squares projects coordinatewise and \(R^2\) is the
captured share of target variance \cite[\S3.2.3]{hastie2009elements}.  Its
role here is structural.  At \(\lambda_{\mathrm{strong}}=0.997\), a
structurally incomplete one-term model earns a deterministic reward of
\(0.997\) in exact arithmetic, while the sibling branch containing the
exact expression returns the far smaller mean \eqref{eq:continue-vroll}
under random completion.  Objectives whose locally averaged signal points
away from the global optimum are called \emph{deceptive} in evolutionary
computation \cite{goldberg1987simple}; the analogous hazard for
sampling-based tree search is the shallow \emph{search trap}
\cite{ramanujan2010adversarial}, on which UCT's transitory regret can grow
doubly exponentially in the depth \cite{coquelin2007bandit}.  The decoy
transplants this structure into single-player symbolic regression, where a
near-perfect \(R^2\) on a wrong functional form is precisely the failure
mode that motivates reporting predictive-accuracy and symbolic solutions
separately in benchmark evaluation \cite{lacava2021contemporary}.
\end{rem}

\paragraph{Continue action.} The second action, denoted \(C\), opens the
compositional branch.  It contains the structurally exact four-component
expression, whose reward is \(1\); a uniform random completion, however,
recovers that expression only rarely, and the mean completion reward is
\(p_M\), cf.\ \eqref{eq:continue-vroll}.

The action creates four atom slots \(A_1,\ldots,A_4\).  Each slot has
the productions
\begin{equation}
    A_r
    \longrightarrow
    \psi_1\mid\psi_2\mid\cdots\mid\psi_M,
    \qquad r=1,\ldots,4,
    \label{eq:atom-productions}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    M\in\{4,12\}.
    \label{eq:M-values}
\end{equation}
A terminal continuation is an ordered tuple
\[
    \mathbf j=(j_1,j_2,j_3,j_4)
    \in\{1,\ldots,M\}^4.
\]
Let
\[
    J(\mathbf j)=\{j_1,j_2,j_3,j_4\}
\]
be the set of distinct selected atoms.  The corresponding fitted model space
is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal M_{\mathbf j}
    =
    \operatorname{span}
    \bigl(
        \{1\}\cup\{\psi_j:j\in J(\mathbf j)\}
    \bigr).
    \label{eq:terminal-model-space}
\end{equation}
Repeated atoms are removed before fitting, or equivalently handled by the
Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse.

For a design matrix \(X_{\mathbf j}\), the fitted coefficients are
\begin{equation}
    \widehat a_{\mathbf j}
    =
    (X_{\mathbf j}^{\mathsf T}X_{\mathbf j})^\dagger
    X_{\mathbf j}^{\mathsf T}y_\lambda.
    \label{eq:least-squares-fit}
\end{equation}
The terminal reward is unpenalized \(R^2\),
\begin{equation}
    R(\mathbf j)
    =
    1-
    \frac{
        \|y_\lambda-X_{\mathbf j}\widehat a_{\mathbf j}\|_2^2
    }{
        \|y_\lambda-\overline y_\lambda\mathbf 1\|_2^2
    }.
    \label{eq:r2-reward}
\end{equation}
No reward clipping or nonlinear optimizer is used.

Repeating the projection argument in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:decoy-reward}, now for the orthonormal basis of
\(\mathcal M_{\mathbf j}\), shows that the reward has the exact form
\begin{equation}
    R(\mathbf j)
    =
    \lambda\,\mathbf 1\{1\in J(\mathbf j)\}
    +
    \frac{1-\lambda}{3}
    \sum_{m=2}^4
    \mathbf 1\{m\in J(\mathbf j)\}.
    \label{eq:analytic-terminal-reward}
\end{equation}
Consequently, all rewards lie in \([0,1]\), distractor atoms
\(\psi_5,\ldots,\psi_M\) capture no target variance, and the exact reward is
independent of numerical fitting pathologies.

\subsection{Exact completion statistics}

Let \(D\) and \(C\) denote the root children corresponding to the decoy and
continue actions.  Since the decoy child admits the single completion of
Proposition~\ref{prop:decoy-reward}, it satisfies
\begin{equation}
    V^*(D)=V^q(D)=\lambda.
    \label{eq:decoy-values}
\end{equation}

\begin{prop}[Continue-branch values]
\label{prop:continue-values}
The continue branch selects four atoms uniformly and independently from the
vocabulary \(\{\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_M\}\).  Its best-achievable reward is
\begin{equation}
    V^*(C)=1,
    \label{eq:continue-vstar}
\end{equation}
and its mean-rollout reward is
\begin{equation}
    V^q(C)
    =
    p_M
    =
    1-\left(1-\tfrac{1}{M}\right)^{4}.
    \label{eq:continue-vroll}
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The reward \eqref{eq:analytic-terminal-reward} is maximal when
\(J(\mathbf j)\supseteq\{1,2,3,4\}\), where every indicator equals one and
\[
    R(\mathbf j)
    =
    \lambda+\frac{1-\lambda}{3}\cdot 3
    =
    1;
\]
the tuple \(\mathbf j=(1,2,3,4)\) achieves this, proving
\eqref{eq:continue-vstar}.  For the mean, take expectations in
\eqref{eq:analytic-terminal-reward} under the uniform completion policy
\(q\):
\[
    V^q(C)
    =
    \lambda\,\Pr_q\!\left(1\in J(\mathbf j)\right)
    +
    \frac{1-\lambda}{3}
    \sum_{m=2}^4
    \Pr_q\!\left(m\in J(\mathbf j)\right).
\]
A fixed atom \(m\) is missed by one slot with probability \(1-\frac1M\),
hence by all four independent slots with probability
\((1-\frac1M)^4\), so \(\Pr_q(m\in J(\mathbf j))=p_M\) for every \(m\).
Substituting gives
\(V^q(C)=\lambda p_M+(1-\lambda)p_M=p_M\).
\end{proof}

Comparing \eqref{eq:decoy-values} with \eqref{eq:continue-vroll}, the
mean-rollout evaluator reverses the correct root ordering exactly when
\begin{equation}
    \lambda>p_M.
    \label{eq:inversion-condition}
\end{equation}

Exact recovery requires the four selected atoms to form a permutation of
\((1,2,3,4)\).  Among the \(M^4\) equally likely ordered tuples, exactly
\(4!\) are permutations of the target, hence
\begin{equation}
    \rho_0(C)
    =
    \Pr_q(R=1\mid C)
    =
    \frac{4!}{M^4}.
    \label{eq:exact-mass}
\end{equation}


\begin{question}
What is the minimum number of rollouts ($K$) we must perform to guarantee, with $95\%$ confidence, that we will see the exact solution at least once?
\end{question}

\begin{prop}[Hit probability and sample complexity]
\label{prop:hit-sample-complexity}
Draw \(K\) independent uniform completions of \(C\).  The probability that
at least one is exact is
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{hit}}(K)
    =
    1-\left(1-\rho_0(C)\right)^K,
    \label{eq:hit-probability}
\end{equation}
and, for \(\delta\in(0,1)\), the guarantee
\(P_{\mathrm{hit}}(K)\ge 1-\delta\) holds if and only if
\begin{equation}
    K
    \;\ge\;
    K_{1-\delta}
    =
    \left\lceil
    \frac{\log\delta}
         {\log(1-\rho_0(C))}
    \right\rceil.
    \label{eq:hit-sample-complexity}
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
A single completion misses the exact expression with probability
\(1-\rho_0(C)\).  By independence, all \(K\) completions miss with
probability \((1-\rho_0(C))^K\), and the complement of that event is
\eqref{eq:hit-probability}.  The guarantee
\(P_{\mathrm{hit}}(K)\ge 1-\delta\) is therefore equivalent to
\[
    \left(1-\rho_0(C)\right)^K\le\delta.
\]
Taking logarithms gives \(K\log(1-\rho_0(C))\le\log\delta\), and dividing
by \(\log(1-\rho_0(C))\), which is negative, reverses the inequality:
\[
    K
    \ge
    \frac{\log\delta}{\log(1-\rho_0(C))}.
\]
The smallest integer satisfying this bound is the ceiling in
\eqref{eq:hit-sample-complexity}.
\end{proof}

\section{Experiment 1: exact value-semantic audit}

\paragraph{Question.}
Does mean random-completion return preserve the ordering induced by
best-achievable reward, and how rare is the exact expression within each
partial derivation?

\paragraph{Hypothesis 1.}
The rollout mean ranks the continue branch above the decoy for
\(\lambda=0.25\) and below the decoy for \(\lambda=0.997\):
\begin{equation}
    V^q(C)>\lambda_{\mathrm{weak}},
    \qquad
    V^q(C)<\lambda_{\mathrm{strong}}.
    \label{eq:hypothesis-one-order}
\end{equation}
Independently, the completion budget required for exact recovery scales as
\begin{equation}
    K_{1-\delta}
    =
    \Theta\left(
        \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\rho_0}
    \right)
    \quad\text{as }\rho_0\rightarrow0.
    \label{eq:hypothesis-one-rarity}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Procedure.}
No stochastic tree search is used in this experiment.  For each of the four
cells, enumerate all \(M^4\) terminal continuations and all partial prefixes
of depths \(0,1,2,3,4\).  For each partial state \(s\), compute exactly
\begin{equation}
    V^*(s),\qquad
    V^q(s),\qquad
    \rho_0(s).
    \label{eq:experiment-one-quantities}
\end{equation}
For a nonterminal state \(s\), define the action selected by the mean-rollout
oracle as
\begin{equation}
    a_q(s)
    =
    \arg\max_a V^q(T(s,a)),
    \label{eq:mean-oracle-action}
\end{equation}
and define its oracle sibling regret by
\begin{equation}
    g_q(s)
    =
    V^*(s)-V^*(T(s,a_q(s))).
    \label{eq:sibling-regret}
\end{equation}
The largest environment contains only
\[
    1+12+12^2+12^3+12^4=22{,}621
\]
continue-prefix states, so exhaustive calculation is tractable.

The implementation must compute each terminal reward in two ways:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item using the analytical expression
    \eqref{eq:analytic-terminal-reward};
    \item using the actual least-squares evaluator
    \eqref{eq:least-squares-fit}--\eqref{eq:r2-reward}.
\end{enumerate}
The two values must agree to numerical tolerance \(10^{-12}\).

\paragraph{Primary outputs.}
Report the exact root quantities
\[
    V^*(D),\quad V^q(D),\quad
    V^*(C),\quad V^q(C),\quad
    \rho_0(C),\quad K_{0.95}.
\]
Plot:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the terminal completion-reward distribution in each cell;
    \item \(V^q(s)\) against \(V^*(s)\), stratified by state depth;
    \item sibling regret \(g_q(s)\) against depth;
    \item the exact hit curve \(1-(1-\rho_0)^K\).
\end{enumerate}

Because Experiment~1 is an exact census, it requires no random seeds,
confidence intervals, or significance tests.  Failure to reproduce the
analytical identities indicates an implementation error and should prevent
proceeding to Experiment~2.

\section{Experiment 2: finite-budget pure-MCTS limits}

\paragraph{Question.}
At matched terminal-evaluation budgets, do the value-order mismatch and
exact-completion rarity identified in Experiment~1 predict the behavior of
actual pure search?

\subsubsection{Search conditions}

We compare three conditions.

\paragraph{Uniform random search.}
Sample complete derivations independently from the uniform grammar policy and
return the best terminal expression observed.

\paragraph{Mean-UCT.}
At node \(s\), select
\begin{equation}
    a_t
    =
    \arg\max_a
    \left[
        \overline Q_t(s,a)
        +
        \sqrt{
            \frac{2\log N_t(s)}
                 {N_t(s,a)}
        }
    \right],
    \label{eq:mean-uct}
\end{equation}
where \(\overline Q_t(s,a)\) is the empirical mean of terminal returns from
simulations passing through \((s,a)\).  Unvisited actions receive infinite
score.  Each simulation selects through the current tree, expands one
unvisited edge, performs one uniform random completion, and backs up the
terminal reward by an arithmetic mean.

\paragraph{Max-UCT diagnostic.}
Define
\begin{equation}
    Q_t^{\max}(s,a)
    =
    \max
    \left\{
        R(\pi_b):
        \pi_b\text{ was sampled through }(s,a)
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:qmax}
\end{equation}
Select using
\begin{equation}
    a_t
    =
    \arg\max_a
    \left[
        Q_t^{\max}(s,a)
        +
        \sqrt{
            \frac{2\log N_t(s)}
                 {N_t(s,a)}
        }
    \right].
    \label{eq:max-uct}
\end{equation}
The tree structure, expansion policy, rollout policy, and exploration term
are otherwise identical to Mean-UCT.  This condition is a causal diagnostic
of the mean-versus-maximum distinction, not a claim that naive maximum backup
is universally optimal.  A calibrated extreme-bandit allocation rule is a
natural subsequent comparison \cite{huang2025improving}.

\subsubsection{Evaluation protocol}

Each search run uses one persistent tree rooted at \(s_0\).  The algorithm
does not commit to a grammar action before the budget is exhausted.  No
network, learned prior, Dirichlet noise, reward clipping, complexity penalty,
or nonlinear optimizer is used.  One simulation contains exactly one
terminal reward evaluation.

Use the terminal-evaluation budgets
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal B
    =
    \{
        16,32,64,128,256,512,
        1024,2048,4096,8192
    \}.
    \label{eq:budget-grid}
\end{equation}
For each of the four benchmark cells and each search condition, use \(100\)
independent seeds.  Run each seed once to \(B_{\max}=8192\) and record all
intermediate checkpoints.  The same base-seed list is used across algorithms.

\subsubsection{Outcomes}

Let \(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_B\) be the terminal expressions observed by budget
\(B\).  Define the best-observed reward
\begin{equation}
    R_B^{\max}
    =
    \max_{1\leq b\leq B}R(\pi_b),
    \label{eq:best-observed-reward}
\end{equation}
the simple regret
\begin{equation}
    r_B
    =
    1-R_B^{\max},
    \label{eq:simple-regret}
\end{equation}
and the exact-recovery indicator
\begin{equation}
    S_B
    =
    \mathbf 1
    \left\{
        R_B^{\max}\geq1-10^{-12}
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:exact-recovery-indicator}
\end{equation}
The time to exact recovery is
\begin{equation}
    T_{\mathrm{exact}}
    =
    \inf\{B:S_B=1\}.
    \label{eq:time-to-recovery}
\end{equation}

Exact recovery is the primary outcome.  Simple regret is secondary because
the incorrect decoy in the strong-decoy cells already has simple regret
\[
    1-\lambda_{\mathrm{strong}}=0.003.
\]
Thus predictive reward alone cannot distinguish near-perfect approximation
from structural recovery.  This distinction is consistent with symbolic
regression benchmarks that report both predictive and exact-recovery
performance \cite{lacava2021contemporary}.

For mechanism diagnosis, record the continue-visit fraction
\begin{equation}
    F_B
    =
    \frac{N_B(s_0,C)}{N_B(s_0)},
    \label{eq:continue-fraction}
\end{equation}
together with the final root recommendation, number of distinct terminal
sequences, number of distinct canonical atom sets, and the best reward found
through each root action.  The final visit-count recommendation is diagnostic
only; the primary search output is the best terminal expression encountered.

\subsubsection{Hypotheses}

\paragraph{Hypothesis 2a: value-semantic effect.}
Mean-UCT will allocate less search to the continue branch under
\(\lambda=0.997\) than under \(\lambda=0.25\), because its exploitation
statistic converges toward \(V^q(C)\), which lies below the strong-decoy
reward.  Max-UCT will be less sensitive to this inversion after observing
high-reward continuations.

\paragraph{Hypothesis 2b: rarity effect.}
For every algorithm and fixed \(\lambda\), exact recovery will be slower for
\(M=12\) than for \(M=4\).  At moderate budgets, recovery will be organized
approximately by the effective rare-event budget
\begin{equation}
    B\rho_0.
    \label{eq:effective-rare-budget}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Hypothesis 2c: interaction.}
The largest recovery difference between Max-UCT and Mean-UCT will occur in
the strong-decoy, high-distraction cell
\[
    (\lambda,M)=(0.997,12),
\]
where the continue branch has both a lower rollout mean than the decoy and a
small exact-completion mass.

One pre-specified interaction contrast is
\begin{align}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{semantic}}(B,M)
    &=
    \left[
        P_B^{\mathrm{Max}}-P_B^{\mathrm{Mean}}
    \right]_{\lambda=0.997}
    \nonumber\\
    &\quad -
    \left[
        P_B^{\mathrm{Max}}-P_B^{\mathrm{Mean}}
    \right]_{\lambda=0.25},
    \label{eq:semantic-interaction}
\end{align}
where
\[
    P_B^A
    =
    \Pr_A(S_B=1)
\]
is the exact-recovery probability for algorithm \(A\).  The prediction is
\[
    \Delta_{\mathrm{semantic}}(B,M)>0,
\]
with a larger effect expected at \(M=12\).

\subsubsection{Statistical reporting and interpretation}

For each budget, cell, and algorithm, report the exact-recovery proportion
with a \(95\%\) Wilson interval, median simple regret with a seed-bootstrap
interval, the empirical survival curve
\[
    \Pr(T_{\mathrm{exact}}>B),
\]
and the continue-visit fraction \(F_B\).

The interpretation is mechanism-based:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If Mean-UCT fails while Max-UCT succeeds, the mean-return statistic
    is causally implicated.
    \item If both methods deteriorate primarily when \(M=12\), completion
    rarity is the dominant limitation.
    \item If random search outperforms Mean-UCT in a strong-decoy cell, the
    tree allocation rule actively suppresses the optimal branch.
    \item If Mean-UCT succeeds despite the exact value-order inversion, UCT
    exploration is sufficient at the tested budgets and the mismatch alone is
    not a complete explanation.
    \item If Max-UCT performs worse in weak-decoy cells, maximum backup is
    overoptimistic and should not be treated as a universal replacement for
    mean backup.
    \item If simple regret is small while exact recovery remains poor, the
    experiment demonstrates a separation between predictive approximation
    and symbolic identification.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Secondary validation and deferred questions}

After the two primary experiments, a nonconfirmatory validation may repeat
the key comparison on centered and variance-normalized versions of the
motivating quadratic targets, using an additive grammar and direct linear
least squares.  The original full grammar, nonlinear constant fitting,
uniform-prior PUCT, learned policy and value heads, and SRBench evaluation
should be introduced only after the controlled mechanisms are understood.

In particular, a subsequent neural experiment should ask whether a learned
policy or value function corrects the pure-search failures identified here,
or instead learns and amplifies the biased targets produced by mean-return
search.  Separating that learning question from the present pure-search study
is necessary for a causal interpretation.
